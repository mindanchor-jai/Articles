Micro-Article 05  /  Intelligence → Rationalisation

The Smarter You Are,
The Better You Explain Bad Decisions.

⚓

The person who thinks least about their decisions
makes reactive ones obviously.
The person who thinks most about them
makes reactive ones convincingly.
The outcome is the same. The story is better.

There is a specific vulnerability that comes with being reflective, well-read, and emotionally literate. You have more tools for understanding your own behaviour. Which also means you have more tools for explaining it in terms your self-image accepts. And when anger is driving a decision, an intelligent person does not simply act. They build a case. A thorough, reasonable, values-aligned case for why this specific reactive decision is actually the considered one.

The case is convincing. That is the problem. A less sophisticated person might catch themselves saying "I just do not want to be near them right now" — which is transparent, and therefore examinable. A more sophisticated person will have three principled reasons why this specific choice reflects their values, their growth, and their understanding of what they deserve. The three reasons are real. They are just not the actual driver. The anger came first. The reasoning was assembled around it.

Intelligence does not make you
less likely to make reactive decisions.
It makes you far better
at hiding them from yourself.

How the Sequence Actually Works

The honest sequence, when anger is operating, goes like this: something happens, the ego registers a threat, a reactive conclusion forms instantly — stay away, don't engage, don't give them anything. Then, approximately half a second later, the intelligent mind starts working. But it is not working to evaluate the decision. The decision is already made. It is working to explain the decision in language that will satisfy your own standards for what a thoughtful person would do.

The reasoning feels like deliberation because it happens in the same mental space where deliberation normally lives. But it is functioning as rationalisation — working backward from a conclusion rather than forward toward one. And the more capable you are of constructing rigorous-sounding reasoning, the harder it is to notice the direction of travel.

The Sequence Laid Out

Anger fires. Conclusion forms: avoid, withdraw, don't engage. Then the mind activates: "I am choosing this because I value my peace, because this person has shown a pattern of behaviour I do not wish to reinforce, because I have learned that engaging in these dynamics is not productive for me." All of that may be true. The question is whether it is the reason the decision was made, or the story that was constructed to justify a decision already made on entirely different grounds. The difference between those two things is everything.

I have sat with this one uncomfortably. I am reasonably good at reasoning about my own behaviour. And I have noticed that when I am angry, the quality of my justifications goes up, not down. I become more articulate about why my reactive choice is actually the principled one. That increase in articulateness is not a sign that I am thinking more clearly. It is a sign I am working harder to close the case.

Certainty under emotional activation
is not a sign of clarity. It is often the opposite.

The Certainty Signal

Here is the specific tell that cuts through the elaborate reasoning: certainty. Genuine deliberation has doubt in it. You weigh things. You consider what you might be missing. You acknowledge that the situation is complex. You arrive at a decision that feels considered but holds its conclusions loosely enough that new information could revise them.

Reactive decisions feel certain. Fast and certain. The case assembles quickly and completely. There is nothing to weigh because the conclusion was already reached before the weighing began. If you notice that your reasoning about a decision involving someone you are angry with arrived at a firm conclusion very quickly and very completely — that speed and completeness is information. It is not evidence that the decision is right. It is evidence that something other than your deliberate reasoning made it.

The more useful question to ask in that state is not "is this reasoning correct?" — because it probably is correct, at least partially. It is: "did this reasoning produce the conclusion, or did the conclusion produce this reasoning?" That question requires a specific kind of honesty that most people would rather not apply. It is much more comfortable to let the reasoning stand as the reason.

The version of this that changed how I think: I once made a decision I was very certain about, reasoned through thoroughly, and felt clean about — and then three weeks later, when the anger had cooled, looked back at it and could see clearly that it had been a reactive decision I had dressed in principled clothing. The decision was not catastrophic. But the certainty I had felt about it was entirely manufactured by the emotional state, not by the reasoning. The reasoning was real. The certainty was borrowed from the anger.

"The question is not whether
the reasoning is correct.
It is whether the reasoning
produced the conclusion —
or the conclusion produced the reasoning."

What to Do With This

The practical implication is a simple rule: when a decision involves someone you are carrying anger toward, slow down specifically at the point where you feel most certain. That certainty is the signal to apply more scrutiny, not less. Ask the sequence question: did this reasoning produce this conclusion, or did this conclusion produce this reasoning? And if you notice the second — if the conclusion was there before the case was built — you do not necessarily have to change the decision. But you should make it again, from a clearer state, before you commit to it fully.

That is the whole intervention. Not avoiding decisions when angry. Not suppressing the anger. Just refusing to let certainty-that-arrived-quickly serve as evidence that the decision was made by you, rather than made for you by an emotional state you had not examined.

Intelligence is a tool.
Anger can use it just as well as you can.
The difference is in who picked up the tool first —
and whether you noticed.

Not after you have forgiven. Right now, with the feeling fully alive — what does an anchored mind do? That is the final piece.
