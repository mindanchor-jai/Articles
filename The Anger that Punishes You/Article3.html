Micro-Article 03  /  Ego → Disguises

Sometimes "Boundaries" Are Just
Anger Wearing Better Clothes.

⚓

The most dangerous version of anger
is not the one that screams.
It is the one that has learned
to sound exactly like wisdom.

Boundaries are real. They are necessary. Used correctly, they are one of the most important tools available for protecting your time, your energy, and the relationships worth protecting. This article is not an argument against them. It is an argument for being honest with yourself about when you are actually using them — and when you are using the word to describe something else entirely.

Because anger, when it is sophisticated enough, does not show up as anger. It shows up as discernment. As principle. As a clear-eyed assessment that this person is simply not worth your energy. It borrows the vocabulary of emotional maturity and uses it as a container for something that is actually reactive, unprocessed, and still very much in charge of your behaviour.

Genuine boundaries protect your interest.
Anger dressed as boundaries
protects your ego.
They look identical from the outside.

How to Tell Which One You Are Actually Using

The distinction lives in one place: the interior process that produced the decision. Genuine boundaries are slow. They emerge from a considered assessment of a pattern of behaviour, your own needs, and what you are willing to accept going forward. They can be explained without requiring the other person to be a villain. They are stable — you made the decision and it holds without constant re-justification.

Reactive distance is fast. It is certain. It feels righteous in a specific way — the kind of righteous that needs the other person to be wrong in order to stay intact. It requires maintenance. Every time you encounter them in a shared context, you feel the need to re-establish the distance, re-tell yourself the story of why, re-confirm that your decision is the correct one. That is not a boundary. That is an ego that has not settled.

I have used the word "boundaries" to describe what was, on closer examination, just avoidance I had not examined. The tell was the maintenance. A real boundary does not require daily upkeep. It does not need the other person to stay wrong so that I can stay comfortable with the decision. When I noticed I was spending mental energy reinforcing the story, I knew the story was holding something it should not have been.

If the boundary needs constant defending,
it is not a boundary. It is a wound.

The Vocabulary Theft

Language shapes what is available for self-examination. When you call reactive avoidance a "boundary," you put it in a category that feels settled and mature — and therefore beyond question. You are not avoidant; you are boundaried. You are not reactive; you are discerning. You are not running from something uncomfortable; you are choosing not to accept what you have decided is unacceptable.

The problem is that this framing closes the question before it can be asked honestly. Once something is labelled as a boundary, examining it starts to feel like self-betrayal — like you are undermining your own standards or giving in to someone who wronged you. The label creates a kind of immunity to self-reflection. And that immunity is exactly what the ego needs to stay in charge without being noticed.

The Test That Cuts Through It

Ask yourself this: if the other person were to sincerely apologise, acknowledge what they did, and show clear evidence of changed behaviour — would your decision change? If the answer is yes, then what you have is not a boundary. It is a response to their behaviour that remains open to revision. That is reasonable. But call it what it is. A boundary, properly defined, is not contingent on their behaviour changing. It is a decision about what you accept, made from your own values — not a punishment held in place until they repent.

This is not a semantic game. It matters because the two things require different responses from you. If what you have is a genuine boundary — a considered decision about what you accept in your life — then you can hold it calmly, without resentment, without needing the other person to be a certain way. If what you have is unprocessed anger that has borrowed the language of boundaries, then the work is not enforcement. The work is the anger itself. And until you do that work, the boundary will keep requiring the maintenance that real boundaries do not need.

The clearest version of this I can offer: I once held what I called a firm principle about not engaging with a certain person, framed it to myself in very clean language about values and standards, and then noticed that every time the situation shifted in their favour I felt something I could only describe as agitated. That agitation was information. Settled decisions do not produce agitation. Unresolved anger produces agitation. The principle was real. The framing was wrong.

"Avoidance that calls itself discernment
is harder to examine.
The label protects it
from the questions it most needs."

What Genuine Discernment Actually Feels Like

When you have genuinely processed something — when the decision about how to relate to a person comes from clarity rather than from an activated emotional state — it has a particular quality. It is quiet. It does not need to be performed. You can be in the same room as the person and feel neither compelled to connect nor compelled to signal your distance. The decision sits in you without requiring anything from the situation to keep it in place.

That is what you are working toward. Not the absence of anger — that is not always available, and it is not always the right goal. But the ability to make decisions about a person from a place that is yours, not theirs. Governed by your values and your interest, not by how the anger needs the situation to look.

A real boundary doesn't need to be explained,
defended, or maintained daily.
If it does, examine what is actually
holding it in place.

Leading Into Episode 4

Once you see that avoidance can disguise itself as principle, the next layer becomes visible: some of what we call "keeping our distance" is not even really about the other person. It is about whether they are watching. Whether they notice. Whether the distance is being registered by an audience — which tells you everything about whether it is actually serving you.
