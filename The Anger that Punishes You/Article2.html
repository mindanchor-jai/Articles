The Person You're Angry At
Isn't Paying the Price.
You Are.

⚓

You carry the weight of the anger.
You pay for every decision it makes.
They went home fine.
You are still here, managing the cost.

There is a specific delusion at the centre of sustained anger toward another person: the belief that your emotional state is costing them something. That your resentment, your avoidance, your cold distance is a form of punishment they are receiving. It rarely is. More often, they are completely unaware. And the cost you think you are imposing on them is being paid entirely by you.

This is not a philosophical point. It is a practical one. Every decision you make from a place of anger — every seat you choose to avoid them, every opportunity you pass because they are adjacent to it, every conversation you shut down, every situation you make harder for yourself to prevent incidentally helping them — those costs are real. They are yours. And they compound.

You think you are punishing them
by making yourself uncomfortable.
That is not punishment.
That is just suffering.

The Asymmetry Nobody Talks About

Here is what is almost always true: the person you are angry with has a version of events in which they are, if not right, at least understandable. They have their own narrative, their own justifications, their own ability to live with what happened. They are not lying in the dark running the same mental loop you are. They are not cataloguing what they owe you. They are making dinner, sending emails, having other conversations, and existing in a world in which your anger registers somewhere between faint background noise and nothing at all.

While you are rerouting your day to avoid them. While you are choosing worse options so you do not have to benefit them. While you are spending energy maintaining a posture of distance that costs you something every time you perform it. The asymmetry is almost perfect. You are the only accountant in this arrangement. And you are the only one being charged.

I have sat with this one for a long time because it is genuinely uncomfortable. The idea that the person I am angry with is simply not thinking about me the way I am thinking about them. That the whole architecture I have built around avoiding them — the rerouting, the preemptive decisions, the energy spent — is invisible to them. That I paid all of it into an account they do not know exists.

Resentment is expensive.
The bill comes to you. Not them.

What the Cost Actually Looks Like
What You Tell Yourself
What Is Actually Happening
The StoryI am maintaining my standards by not engaging with them.
The RealityYou are passing on options that would serve you because they are connected to someone you dislike.
The StoryI am protecting my energy by keeping my distance.
The RealityYou are spending energy daily managing the distance — rerouting, preempting, justifying.
The StoryI am making clear that their behaviour has consequences.
The RealityThey have not noticed. The consequences are playing out entirely in your own life.
The StoryChoosing not to engage is strength.
The RealityIf the choice consistently makes your situation worse, it is not strength. It is ego maintaining a posture.

None of this means the anger is wrong. What they did may genuinely have been harmful, thoughtless, or worth being angry about. That is separate. The question here is not whether the anger is justified. The question is whether carrying it — and letting it direct your decisions — is serving you. And the honest answer, in most cases, is no. It is serving the need to feel like you are doing something about it. That need and your actual interest are not the same thing.

The clearest version of this I ever saw: someone I knew rearranged a significant part of their professional life to avoid working near a person they had fallen out with. The avoidance cost them real opportunity. The other person was promoted twice in the same period and, as far as anyone could tell, had never given the situation a second thought. One person paid everything. The other paid nothing. The one who paid was the one who thought the anger was a form of power.

"In trying not to be near the person you dislike,
you often end up hurting yourself
far more than they ever could."

What You Get Back When You Stop Paying

The goal here is not peace with them. Not reconciliation. Not even indifference in the warm sense. It is something more immediate and more useful: getting your decision-making back. Returning the governance of your choices to your own interest rather than to the emotional account you have been maintaining on their behalf.

When you stop letting anger make your logistical decisions — when you choose the better seat even if it is near them, take the opportunity even if they are adjacent to it, make the choice that serves you regardless of what it incidentally costs or benefits them — something shifts. Not the relationship. Not the history. Just your own life, making slightly better decisions because the person driving is you again, not the anger.

That is what you get back. Not peace in the abstract. Clarity in the specific. The ability to make the next decision from your own interest rather than from theirs.

They went home fine.
You paid the whole bill.
The anger was never going to make them pay.
It was always going to cost you.
