The Magnitude Mismatch

A small thing happened. The reaction was not small. That gap is the first clue — not proof that someone overreacted, but a sign that something else was already there.

Why does a trivial incident sometimes cause a severe emotional or behavioural blowup?

Fig. 1 — the event, measured against the reaction it supposedly caused.

the surface storywhat everyone sees first

A dish is left in the sink. Someone is five minutes late. A text goes unanswered for an hour longer than usual. Small things — the kind that happen every week without incident.

Then, one day, one of them causes an explosion.

The person reacting may not fully understand it either. "I don't know why that got to me so much," they say afterward — not as an excuse, but as a genuine question.

That question deserves a real answer. The size of a reaction is information. And right now, it doesn't match the size of what caused it.

what the trigger can't explain

The Reaction Was Never About the Dish

Human reactions are often driven by background strain that has nothing to do with the moment in front of them. The size of a reaction does not tell you the size of its trigger — it tells you how much was already stored before the trigger arrived.

Before noticing the gap

The trigger explains everything. A small comment caused a big reaction, so the comment must have been the real problem.

After noticing the gap

The trigger explains the timing, not the size. Something else was already loaded — the trigger only released it.

you've probably heard thisor said it yourself

How This Shows Up

The gap between a small trigger and a big reaction has a familiar shape.

  • Someone says, "It wasn't about the dishes — it was about everything else."
  • A minor inconvenience produces rage that surprises even the person feeling it.
  • Onlookers say, "That wasn't even a big deal," while the reaction keeps going.
  • Afterward, the person feels drained — the way you do after carrying something heavy, not after one small moment.
not a verdict on anyone

A reaction that outsizes its trigger isn't proof someone overreacted. It's proof something was already there, waiting for a way out.
