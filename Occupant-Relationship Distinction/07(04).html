An Inequality Cannot Be Understood From One Number Alone

An observable gap tells you that something is different. It does not, by itself, tell you why — and a correction aimed at the wrong cause can create a new unfairness instead of fixing the old one.

Central question

Are we examining the actual cause of an inequality, or drawing a conclusion from only one part of the picture?

Fig. 4 — the same prize money, set against two different totals, tells two different stories.

Where you may recognize this

This shows up whenever one figure is made to carry an entire verdict:

  • Two payments are compared side by side, and the gap is treated as proof of discrimination before anyone asks what produced it.
  • A single statistic ends the conversation instead of opening it.
  • Someone who sees only one side of a number feels certain that whatever doesn't go their way must be wrong.

What one number leaves out

At the 2022 men's World Cup, FIFA set aside $440 million in prize money; at the 2023 Women's World Cup, $110 million — about a quarter. Put side by side, the conclusion feels immediate.

Now ask what each tournament brought in. Men's 2022 revenue across its four-year cycle was about $7.5 billion; the 2023 Women's tournament generated more than $570 million. Measured as a share of revenue, the men's prize money was about 6% of what was earned; the women's was about 19% — a larger share paid out of less.

Neither number is false. They answer different questions. The point is not who was right — it is that each view, taken alone, was incomplete, and a correction built on an incomplete view can land on the wrong cause.

One number

$440M vs. $110M in prize money. Conclusion: women were paid far less.

Set against revenue

~6% vs. ~19% of what was earned. Conclusion: a different, more complicated story.

What becomes visible

The gap between observing an unequal outcome and understanding the conditions that produced it. A correction aimed at the wrong cause does not remove an injustice — it can create a new one, somewhere a true picture would not have pointed.

A boundary carried from the source

These percentages are rough — the men's figure spans a four-year cycle, not one tournament, and the two tournaments' revenues were historically sold together and hard to separate. This does not settle whether women's football receives fair investment or promotion, which can itself shape what it earns. It shows how a single number can mislead; it does not show that pay gaps are fair, and many real ones are discrimination that deserve to be examined case by case.

Reader understanding

A difference is something to investigate, not a complete explanation of itself. Seeing the whole picture is what lets a correction land where the problem actually is.

Think of a gap you've seen reduced to a single number. What question would the full picture have let you ask instead?
