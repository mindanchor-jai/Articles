Correcting Injustice Is Not the Same as Retaliation

A real grievance is a legitimate reason to seek correction. It is not, by itself, proof that any particular response to that grievance is fair.

Central question

Does an action become fair simply because it is meant to correct an earlier injustice?

Fig. 3 — removing a barrier and imposing a new one can look like the same gesture from a distance.

Where you may recognize this

The slide from correction into retaliation usually arrives dressed as justice:

  • Someone argues a person should lose an opportunity specifically because they belong to a group that historically held an advantage.
  • A measure removes an old, unfair restriction — versus a measure that creates a new, unrelated one aimed at a different group.
  • “They had it coming” is offered as the entire justification for a new exclusion.

Two separate questions, often asked as one

Historical injustice creates a legitimate reason to seek correction. Removing a restriction, changing an unfair rule, or designing a justified measure to address a real disadvantage can all be part of a legitimate response.

But suppose the proposed response is instead to exclude someone simply because they belong to the group that historically benefited. The original injustice does not automatically establish the fairness of that decision. A group's historical position cannot, by itself, determine the moral responsibility of every individual inside it.

The legitimacy of the grievance and the legitimacy of the specific response are different questions, and have to be examined separately.

Correction

Removes the barrier that caused the original unfairness. Aimed at the condition.

Retaliation

Imposes an unrelated restriction on a different group. Aimed at a person, for what their group once did.

What becomes visible

The purpose of correction is to address an injustice; the purpose of dominance is to establish superiority. A response does not become just merely because it comes from someone who has previously experienced injustice — and it does not become unjust merely because it changes an existing distribution of power. Purpose, design, and consequence are what decide it.

Where this fits

In the manifestation

This names one specific way a corrective attempt can quietly reproduce the very relationship it claims to be dismantling.

In the model

It sharpens the Relationship and Conditions lenses by separating barrier removal from group-based retaliation — one of the model's governing distinctions.

Reader understanding

A past injustice does not make every subsequent action fair. The purpose, circumstances, and consequences of a corrective action matter as much as the grievance behind it.

Has a correction you've supported or opposed actually targeted the unfair condition — or did it target a person, for what their group once did?
