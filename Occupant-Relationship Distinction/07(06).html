Same Seat, Different Height: Why Fairness Is Not Always Identical Treatment

Identical treatment can leave real differences exactly where they were. Fitting the response to the actual barrier is what levels the table — and there is still a line between levelling it and tilting it the other way.

Central question

When does fairness require different treatment rather than identical treatment — and where does levelling end and tilting begin?

Fig. 6 — three identical chairs, three different views of the table.

Where you may recognize this

The gap between equal and identical shows up anywhere people start from different positions:

  • Everyone at a table is given the exact same chair — a tall adult, a child, a wheelchair user — and only one of them can actually see over the table.
  • A doctor prescribes the same pill to every patient regardless of what is actually wrong with them.
  • A policy that treats everyone “the same” quietly leaves the same gap in place that existed before it.

Levelling the table, and tilting it

A host gives every guest at a long table exactly the same chair. It sounds perfectly fair — until you notice who is sitting there: a tall adult, a child, a person in a wheelchair. The seats are equal. The height at which each person meets the table is not.

What matters in the end is whether each person can reach the food, join the conversation, and be seen — even if that means the chairs are not all the same. Equal seats are one thing. Equal height at the table, once everyone is sitting, is another.

But the same picture shows where the limit sits. If one guest's chair is raised so high they now look down on everyone else, the table is not level — it has only been tilted the other way. Fitting a chair to a real difference is equality. Lifting one guest above the table is dominance in a different seat.

Levelling

The adjustment is sized to an actual, specific barrier. Once seated, everyone meets the table at roughly the same height.

Tilting

The adjustment overshoots the barrier. One guest now looks down on the rest — the table has a new slope, not none.

What becomes visible

That identical treatment and fair treatment are not the same claim, and that the correction is only as sound as its fit to the real difference it is addressing.

A boundary carried from the source

This picture does not say how high any particular chair should be in a real situation. That depends on what is actually being addressed, why the adjustment is needed, how it is designed, and what it causes. The table shows the idea — it does not replace examining each case on its own.

Reader understanding

Identical treatment is not always fair when people start from different positions. Levelling access is one thing; raising one party above everyone else is a different arrangement wearing the language of fairness.

Think of a rule applied identically to everyone. Did it actually level anything, or did it just apply the same height of chair to very different people?
