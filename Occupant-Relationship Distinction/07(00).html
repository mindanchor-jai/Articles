The Problem Was Never Who Holds the Power

On Male Dominance, Feminism, and the Meaning of Equality

Central Thesis

Genuine equality requires dismantling the structure of dominance rather than transferring its authority from one group to another. Changing who holds power does not necessarily change how power is exercised.

01

The Buffet: A Simple Question About Fairness

Imagine a five-star hotel organising a grand buffet. The advertisement is clear:

"Free Buffet for All."

The event begins. Guests arrive. They are well-dressed, confident, and comfortable in their surroundings. They approach the entrance and are allowed inside without difficulty.

Nobody questions them. Nobody asks them to prove that they belong.

Then another man arrives.

His trousers are torn. His shirt is dirty. His appearance is very different from that of the other guests.

As he approaches the entrance, security stops him.

He is not allowed to enter.

You are watching this situation from a distance. What exactly is wrong?

Is it that the hotel has allowed some people to enter while excluding another person? Is it that the man does not meet the appearance expected of the other guests? Or is it that an institution which promised equal access has applied different standards to different people?

Now imagine that you have the authority to change the situation. You have three choices.

  • You can remove the guests who have already entered and allow the excluded man to take their place.
  • You can open the entrance equally, allowing everyone who is entitled to attend to enter without arbitrary discrimination.
  • You can leave the situation unchanged.

Which option would you choose?

The second option seems the fairest. Notice why.

You are not asking the hotel to remove the people who have already entered. You are not asking it to make the excluded man superior to everyone else.

You are asking it to honour the promise it made in the first place.

You are asking for equal access.

This simple distinction leads to a much larger question about society, gender relations, and the meaning of equality.

02

Understanding the Historical Imbalance

For a considerable part of human history, women have faced restrictions that men were more readily able to avoid.

Their access to education, employment, property ownership, financial independence, political participation, and personal decision-making has often been limited by laws, social expectations, and institutional arrangements.

These were not simply differences in individual circumstances. They reflected broader social arrangements in which men, as a group, held greater authority and access to opportunities.

The struggle against these restrictions is an important part of the history of feminism. Women have had to challenge practices and institutions that treated them as less capable, less independent, or less entitled to participate in society.

The demand for equal rights, dignity, opportunity, and freedom is legitimate. There should be no confusion about this.

The purpose of examining the relationship between equality and dominance is not to question whether women deserve equality. They do. Nor is it to dismiss the historical disadvantages women have experienced. Those disadvantages are an essential part of understanding why corrective action became necessary.

The more difficult question begins somewhere else.

When we correct an unequal relationship, are we changing the relationship itself, or are we only changing who occupies its more powerful position?

These are not necessarily the same thing.

03

Equality and Dominance Are Different Objectives

It is easy to confuse equality with a change in the distribution of power.

When one group has historically enjoyed greater authority, correcting that imbalance may naturally involve changing existing arrangements. Women may need access to opportunities from which they were previously excluded. Institutions may need to change their practices. Existing advantages may need to be examined and corrected.

These changes can be necessary for meaningful equality.

However, there is an important distinction between correcting an unfair arrangement and establishing a new arrangement in which another group enjoys superiority.

Consider the buffet again. There are three different possibilities: the existing exclusion continues; the excluded person is allowed to replace the people already inside; or the institution removes the unfair restriction and allows equal participation.

Only the third directly addresses the original problem without creating a different form of exclusion. The first preserves the injustice. The second changes who benefits from the arrangement but does not necessarily correct the principle governing it. The third changes the conditions under which people are treated.

This is the difference between equality and dominance.

Equality concerns the fair recognition of rights, dignity, opportunities, and participation. Dominance concerns a relationship in which one group exercises superiority over another.

The two should not be confused simply because both involve power.

A person can exercise authority without dominating another group. A group can gain greater representation without establishing superiority. A corrective measure can change existing arrangements without necessarily becoming an act of discrimination.

The important question is not whether power has moved. It is what kind of relationship that power now creates.

04

What Happens When We Only Change the Occupants?

Consider a building with a single entrance controlled by a gatekeeper. For years, the gatekeeper has allowed members of one group to enter while placing additional restrictions on another.

The excluded group eventually challenges the arrangement. The gatekeeper loses authority.

Now imagine that a member of the previously excluded group takes control of the entrance. What happens next depends on the principle governing the new arrangement.

The new gatekeeper could open access fairly. Or the new gatekeeper could impose restrictions on the group that previously enjoyed unrestricted entry.

In the second situation, the identities have changed, but the relationship remains recognisable. There is still a gatekeeper. There is still a group whose access depends on another group's approval. There is still an arrangement in which one group can exercise arbitrary control over another.

The people occupying these positions have changed. The underlying relationship has not necessarily changed.

This is the central problem with hierarchy reversal.

A hierarchy is not eliminated simply because its occupants are replaced. If superiority remains the organising principle, the structure can survive under a different identity.

This does not mean that every redistribution of power is a hierarchy reversal. Nor does it mean that every corrective measure is an attempt to establish superiority.

It means that a change in who benefits from an arrangement is not, by itself, sufficient evidence that the arrangement has become fair. We have to examine the relationship that has been created.

05

The Difference Between Corrective Justice and Retaliation

There is another distinction that becomes important here. Historical injustice creates a legitimate reason to seek correction. But the legitimacy of the original grievance and the legitimacy of every possible response to it are separate questions.

Suppose a person has been unfairly denied an opportunity because of their gender. Correcting that injustice is reasonable. Removing the restriction, changing the unfair rule, or creating a justified measure to address an existing disadvantage may all form part of a legitimate response.

But suppose the proposed response is to exclude another individual simply because they belong to the group that historically benefited from the arrangement.

The original injustice does not automatically establish the fairness of that decision.

The historical position of a group cannot, by itself, determine the moral responsibility of every individual belonging to it.

This distinction matters because collective historical experience and individual accountability operate at different levels.

A group may have historically benefited from an unequal structure without every member having personally created, supported, or benefited equally from it. Likewise, a person belonging to a historically disadvantaged group does not automatically become justified in every action taken in the name of correcting that disadvantage.

Justice requires examining the actual situation. What was the original disadvantage? What is the proposed correction intended to achieve? Who is affected? Is the measure addressing an unfair barrier, or is it establishing an unrelated restriction? What are its actual consequences?

These questions cannot be answered simply by identifying which group is historically disadvantaged and which group is historically advantaged.

Corrective justice and retributive dominance are not interchangeable. The purpose of correction is to address an injustice. The purpose of dominance is to establish superiority.

A response does not become just merely because it comes from someone who has previously experienced injustice. Equally, a response does not become unjust merely because it changes an existing distribution of power. The purpose, context, design, and consequences matter.

06

Seeing the Whole Picture Before We Judge

So far, the buffet has given us a clear case. The promise was equal access, and one man was refused. Most real situations are not that clear. Often the unfairness we see depends on how much of the situation we can see.

Take a pay debate from world football.

At the 2022 men's World Cup, FIFA set aside $440 million in prize money. At the 2023 Women's World Cup, the prize money was $110 million, about a quarter of the men's amount. Put those two numbers side by side and the conclusion feels immediate: women were paid far less. For many people, that is where the story ends. And from that view, it is easy to call it dominance. Men hold the power, so men are paid more.

Now ask one more question. How much money did each tournament bring in?

FIFA reported record revenue of about $7.5 billion for the four-year period around the 2022 men's World Cup. For the 2023 Women's World Cup, its president said the tournament generated more than $570 million. Set the prize money against those figures, and the picture changes.

Measured this way, the women's tournament paid out a larger share of what it earned than the men's did. The first view says: women were paid less. The second says: what was earned was paid, and then some. Neither number is false. They answer different questions.

The point is not to say who was right. It is that each view, taken alone, was incomplete. A person who hears only one side feels that anything not going their way must be wrong. A person who sees the whole picture may find that the situation is not what it first looked like, and that the adjustment needed is smaller, or different, or falls on more than one side. It is not an adjustment for one gender to make. Incomplete knowledge is the enemy here, more than any group.

Before we call something dominance, we should ask whether we are looking at the whole situation or only our side of it.
Limits of this example These percentages are rough. The men's figure is the revenue for a four-year cycle, not the tournament alone, and the women's figure is FIFA's own statement. For years FIFA also sold the rights to both tournaments together, so the two revenues were hard to separate. Prize money mostly goes to national federations, not straight to players. And this example does not settle whether women's football gets fair investment or promotion, which may itself shape how much it earns. It shows how a single number can mislead. It does not show that pay gaps are fair, and many real ones are discrimination and should be examined one by one.

This links back to the thesis. If we treat every gap as dominance, we may aim our correction at the wrong cause. A correction aimed at the wrong cause does not remove an injustice. It can create a new one. Seeing the whole picture is what lets a correction land where the problem actually is.

07

Why the Cycle of Dominance Is Worth Questioning

There is a possible consequence worth examining.

Imagine a society in which one group holds greater authority and uses that position to restrict another group. The disadvantaged group challenges the arrangement. The existing structure begins to change.

But suppose the objective eventually shifts from securing equal participation to establishing superiority over the group that previously held power. The original relationship may then be reproduced under different circumstances. The group identities have changed, but the underlying idea remains:

One group should have authority over another.

If this happens, the original injustice has not been fully resolved. The direction of dominance has changed, but dominance itself remains.

This raises the possibility of a repeating pattern:

A possible sequenceUnequal arrangement → resistance → correction → entanglement with demands for reciprocal superiority → new grievance → further resistance
A boundary This is a possible conceptual sequence, not an established universal pattern. The material examined here does not demonstrate that contemporary feminism generally follows this progression, how frequently such a transition occurs, or provide specific real-world cases proving that it has occurred. It would be inaccurate to describe this cycle as an inevitable consequence of equality movements.

The value of considering it lies elsewhere. It helps identify a question that should remain relevant whenever an unequal arrangement is being corrected:

Are we removing the conditions that created the injustice, or are we creating the possibility of another injustice under a different identity?

That question deserves examination without assuming its answer in advance.

08

Acknowledging the Past Without Reproducing Its Injustices

One possible objection to this argument is understandable. Women have experienced generations of disadvantage. Why should we be concerned about the possibility of dominance changing hands when the original imbalance has existed for so long?

The question deserves a serious response.

Historical injustice cannot be dismissed simply because the circumstances are different today. Nor can the consequences of past exclusion be assumed to disappear immediately when a formal restriction is removed.

Correcting historical inequality may require more than treating everyone identically. Where unequal starting conditions or continuing barriers exist, different forms of treatment may be justified.

Equality does not always mean identical treatment.

For example, if an institution has maintained an unfair barrier for many years, removing that barrier may not be enough to ensure meaningful access. A justified corrective measure may be necessary. Such a measure is not automatically an attempt to establish dominance. Its legitimacy depends on what it is addressing, why it is necessary, how it is designed, and what consequences it produces.

The argument is therefore not that existing arrangements should remain untouched in the name of neutrality. It is not that historically advantaged groups should retain every advantage. And it is not that every intervention benefiting a disadvantaged group is unfair to others.

The argument is simpler. We can recognise historical injustice without making the reproduction of injustice our method of correction. We can change unfair arrangements without assuming that another group must lose its legitimate participation. We can recognise collective disadvantage without assigning collective guilt to every individual.

The past explains why correction may be necessary. It does not, by itself, determine whether every proposed correction is justified.

09

Equal Seats Are Not the Same as Equal Height

Picture a long table where everyone has been invited to sit and eat. The host gives every guest exactly the same chair, the same height, the same cushion. It sounds perfectly fair.

Now notice who is sitting there. A tall adult. A child. A person in a wheelchair. Each has the same chair. But once they sit down, they are not at the same height. The adult looks down at the plate. The child can barely see over the edge of the table.

The seats were equal. The ability to take part was not.

Equal seats are one thing. Equal height at the table, once everyone is sitting, is another.

What matters in the end is the second one. A fair table is one where each person can reach the food, join the conversation, and be seen, even if that means the chairs are not all the same.

This is what it means to say that equality is not the same as identical treatment. Having the same rights on paper is where equality starts. It is not where it ends. People are different, and those differences are not a problem to be removed. Equality has to work with them.

Medicine offers a simple way to see this. One medicine does not cure every illness. And even for the same illness, the same medicine does not work equally well in every body. A doctor who gives everyone the same pill is treating everyone the same way, but is not caring for everyone equally. Care means fitting the treatment to the person, so that each one gets the same chance of getting well.

The same applies to fairness between groups. Where people start from different positions, treating them identically can leave the gap exactly where it was. A justified adjustment is not a favour to one side. It is how the table becomes level.

But the same picture also shows where the limit is. If the host raises one guest's chair so high that this guest now looks down on everyone else, the table is no longer level. It has only been tilted the other way. That is the difference this article has been following from the start: levelling the table is equality. Lifting someone above the table is dominance in a different seat.

A boundary This picture does not tell us how high each chair should be in any real situation. That depends on what is actually being addressed, why the adjustment is needed, how it is designed, and what it causes. The table shows the idea. It does not replace the examination of each case.
10

A Society That Does Not Need a Dominant Gender

Men and women are not opposing forces that must compete for permanent control over society. They are individuals sharing families, communities, institutions, and social life.

Their experiences are not identical. Their circumstances are not always the same. They may encounter different challenges and forms of disadvantage. Recognising these differences does not require establishing superiority.

Nor does acknowledging the historical dominance of men require treating every man as inherently guilty.

A society concerned with genuine equality must be able to recognise injustice regardless of who commits it and who experiences it. Women should not have to justify their right to dignity, independence, opportunity, or participation. Men should not be treated as automatically undeserving of dignity or legitimate participation because they belong to the group that historically held greater social power.

These positions are not contradictory. They are compatible with the same underlying principle: human dignity should not depend on group identity.

The objective should not be to create a male-dominated society. Nor should it be to create a female-dominated society.

It should be to create conditions in which neither gender needs to establish superiority over the other to secure its rights and opportunities.

This is also why equality should not be understood as a limited resource. One person's dignity does not require another person's humiliation. One group's participation does not automatically require another group's exclusion. Greater opportunity for women does not, by definition, mean diminished legitimacy for men. And the correction of historical disadvantages does not require the creation of new, unjustified disadvantages.

The real question is whether society can move beyond organising relationships around superiority in the first place.

11

What Should Feminism Stand For?

Feminism remains important wherever women face discrimination, unjust restrictions, or unequal opportunities. Its historical contribution to challenging the denial of women's rights should not be confused with every expression of gender-based discourse that may arise around it.

There is a distinction between a movement's foundational purpose and particular arguments, interpretations, or actions expressed in its name.

Criticising a particular expression of dominance does not invalidate the broader struggle for women's equality. Equally, the legitimacy of feminism does not require accepting every position expressed under its banner without examination.

The standard should be consistent. When women are denied equal rights, that injustice deserves to be challenged. When institutions maintain unfair barriers, those barriers deserve examination. When corrective measures are justified, they should not be rejected simply because they change existing arrangements. And when any person or group seeks superiority through arbitrary exclusion, that should also be questioned.

This responsibility does not belong to women alone. Men have a role in recognising historical and continuing inequalities, questioning unfair advantages, and supporting equal participation. Women and men alike have a responsibility to distinguish between justice and retaliation.

The wider responsibility belongs to everyone who influences the institutions, beliefs, and social arrangements through which people live.

The objective is not to decide which gender deserves greater authority. It is to question why gender should determine who is entitled to exercise superiority over another in the first place.

12

Looking Again at the Buffet

Let us return to the hotel.

The advertisement promised a free buffet for everyone. The well-dressed guests entered. The poorly dressed man was stopped.

The unfairness did not lie in the appearance of the people who were admitted. It lay in the unequal standard applied to the person who was excluded.

Now imagine that the hotel responds by removing every guest who entered before him. The man is finally allowed inside.

But what has actually changed?

The original guests have lost their access. The excluded man has taken their place. Yet the institution has not necessarily changed the principle by which it decides who belongs. The authority to exclude remains. Only its direction may have changed.

The more meaningful solution was to examine the unfair admission rule and remove the arbitrary restriction.

There may be real situations in which correcting unequal access requires additional measures. The buffet analogy does not provide a complete policy for every such situation. But it makes one conceptual distinction clear.

Replacing the people who benefit from an unfair arrangement is not the same as correcting the arrangement itself.

This distinction takes us back to the original question about feminism and gender equality.

Are we trying to create a society in which women no longer experience the injustices historically imposed upon them? Or are we moving towards a society in which the same relationship of superiority continues, but with different people occupying its positions?

These are fundamentally different objectives.

Seeks equalityRisks preserving dominance
Removes the conditions that allowed one group to dominate anotherKeeps the same relationship of superiority, with different people occupying its positions

We should be able to acknowledge the injustices of the past, correct the disadvantages that continue into the present, and question any new form of unjustified exclusion that may emerge. None of these responsibilities requires us to choose between the dignity of men and the dignity of women.

Perhaps the most important shift is to stop evaluating equality only by asking who holds power. Instead, we should examine the relationship through which that power is exercised.

Do we have the whole picture, or only our side of it? Who has access? Who is excluded? What justifies the difference? Does the arrangement correct an existing injustice, or does it create a new form of group-based superiority? And does the institution or relationship allow people to participate without requiring one group to remain subordinate to another?

These questions lead us towards a more accurate understanding of equality.

The problem was never simply that one gender held greater power. The deeper problem was the relationship that allowed power to become a means of establishing superiority over another.

Changing the occupants of that relationship may change who benefits. It does not necessarily change the relationship itself.

Genuine equality is not achieved by changing who gets to dominate. It is achieved by changing the conditions that allow dominance to exist.

And that is why equality must remain the destination, rather than dominance in a different form.
